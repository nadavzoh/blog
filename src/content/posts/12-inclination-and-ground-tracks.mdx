## What inclination means

**Inclination** is the tilt of the orbital plane relative to Earth's equator.

- **0°** — equatorial: the satellite stays over the equator the whole time.
- **90°** — polar: the orbit passes over both poles, eventually overflying every
  point on Earth as the planet rotates beneath it.
- **Between** — the orbit reaches as far north and south as its inclination, but
  never higher. A 51.6° orbit (the ISS) never passes over anywhere above 51.6°
  latitude.

This is a hard geometric ceiling, and it explains a launch fact: you can't reach
an inclination *lower* than your launch site's latitude without an expensive
plane change. It also shapes constellations — a whole family of orbits at the
same inclination weaves the repeating patterns you see draped over the globe.

## The ground track

Trace the point on Earth directly beneath a satellite as it orbits and you get a
**ground track**. Because the satellite swings north and south while Earth spins
west-to-east underneath, the track draws a leaning **sine wave** across the map.
Its height is set by the inclination; each successive lap is shifted westward
because the planet has rotated during one orbit.

A few inclinations earn special names:

- **Equatorial (0°)** — a flat line along the equator; where the
  [geostationary belt](/posts/09-geostationary-orbits/) lives.
- **ISS (51.6°)** — high enough to overfly most of the populated world, low
  enough to launch to easily from several spaceports.
- **Polar (90°)** — sweeps pole to pole; perfect for imaging every spot on Earth.
- **Sun-synchronous (~98°)** — a slightly *retrograde* tilt chosen so that
  Earth's equatorial bulge slowly rotates the orbit plane by exactly 360° per
  year. The orbit then keeps a fixed angle to the Sun, so the satellite crosses
  the equator at the same **local solar time** on every pass — ideal for imagery
  with consistent lighting.

## Putting it all together

That sun-synchronous trick is the perfect closing note, because it uses the very
"imperfection" — Earth's bulge — that forced us to use [SGP4](/posts/11-sgp4-propagation/)
instead of a textbook ellipse, and *turns it into a feature*.

Across these five posts we have gone from the satellite viewer feeling like magic
to understanding every part of it:

- satellites live in distinct **[regimes](/posts/08-orbital-regimes/)** — LEO,
  MEO, and the **[geostationary](/posts/09-geostationary-orbits/)** ring;
- each orbit is captured by six **[orbital elements](/posts/10-orbital-elements-and-tles/)**,
  shipped as a **TLE**;
- **[SGP4](/posts/11-sgp4-propagation/)** turns that snapshot into a live
  position, the engine inside the viewer;
- and **inclination** tilts the plane, drawing the ground tracks that pattern the
  whole globe.

gravity, geometry, and a handful of honest numbers, falling around the planet
exactly as we've described since [post one](/posts/01-what-is-gravity/).
