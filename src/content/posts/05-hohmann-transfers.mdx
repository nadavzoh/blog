We've reached the payoff. You understand gravity, what an orbit is, how fast you
must go, and the elliptical shapes orbits take. Now let's *navigate*: how do you
move a spacecraft from one circular orbit up to a higher one — using as little
fuel as possible?

The classic answer is the **Hohmann transfer orbit**, and its beauty is that it
needs just **two engine burns.**

## Fuel is everything: meet delta-v

In space there's no air and no friction, so you don't burn fuel to *keep moving*
— you only burn fuel to *change* your motion. The total amount of velocity change
a mission requires is called its **delta-v** (written $\Delta v$). Think of
delta-v as the true "distance" between orbits. Every drop of propellant a rocket
carries buys a fixed budget of delta-v, so smart mission design means spending it
wisely.

How much delta-v a given tank of fuel actually buys is set by **Tsiolkovsky's
rocket equation**:

$$
\Delta v = v_{\text{e}} \ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)
$$

Here $v_{\text{e}}$ is the exhaust speed of the engine, $m_0$ the fully fuelled
mass, and $m_f$ the mass once the tanks are dry. The logarithm is the cruel part:
because delta-v grows only with the *log* of the mass ratio, each extra bit of
delta-v costs exponentially more propellant. That is exactly why mission
designers fight so hard to shave delta-v — and why the fuel-sipping maneuvers
below matter so much.

## The two-burn dance

A Hohmann transfer connects a small circular orbit to a larger one using **half
of an ellipse** that just touches both:

1. **Burn 1 — speed up.** A short prograde (forward) burn raises the *far* side
   of your orbit until it reaches the target altitude. You're now coasting along
   a transfer ellipse, climbing outward and naturally slowing down (remember
   Kepler's second law — far means slow).
2. **Coast.** Engines off. Gravity does all the work for half an orbit.
3. **Burn 2 — speed up again.** Arriving at the high point, you're moving too
   slowly for a circular orbit way out here, so a second prograde burn
   *circularises* you into the new orbit.

## Why "half an ellipse" is the cheap option

It feels backwards that you speed *up* to climb to a higher orbit, even though
higher orbits move *slower* overall (from
[post two](/posts/02-newtons-cannonball/), $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$). The resolution:
your first burn trades speed for *altitude*. As you coast outward, gravity bleeds
away that extra speed, so by the time you arrive you're actually moving slower
than before — which is why you need that second burn to top back up.

To put real numbers on those burns we need the speed at *any* point of an orbit,
not just a circular one. That comes from the **vis-viva equation**, which follows
straight from conservation of energy:

$$
v^2 = G M\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)
$$

where $r$ is the current distance from the planet's centre and $a$ is the
semi-major axis of the orbit you're on. For a circle $r = a$ and it collapses
back to $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$; for the transfer ellipse, $a$ is the average of the
inner and outer radii. Each Hohmann burn is just the *difference* between the
circular speed and the transfer-ellipse speed at that radius.

The total cost is the sum of the two burns:

$$
\Delta v_{\text{total}} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2
$$

For most journeys between nearby circular orbits, no two-burn maneuver beats the
Hohmann transfer on fuel. It's literally how we send probes to Mars: wait for the
planets to line up, burn once to leave Earth's orbit around the Sun, coast for
months along a transfer ellipse, then burn again to arrive.

## Where to from here

From a single idea — gravity falling off as $1/r^2$ — we've built our way to
interplanetary travel:

- **Gravity** pulls everything toward a centre, weakening with the square of
  distance.
- **Orbits** are continuous free-fall: falling sideways fast enough to miss.
- **Orbital and escape speeds** tell us how fast "fast enough" is.
- **Kepler's laws** give orbits their elliptical shape and rhythm.
- **Hohmann transfers** let us hop between orbits — and between worlds.

That's the core of orbital mechanics. But two beautiful tricks remain. The
Hohmann transfer spends fuel to climb; next we'll see how to gain speed for
*free* by swinging past a moving planet.
