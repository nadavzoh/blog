We just saw how to swing *past* a planet for free speed. Now for the opposite
trick: how to **stop** relative to two bodies at once and stay there with almost
no effort. The answer is one of the most elegant results in all of orbital
mechanics — the five **Lagrange points.**

## The problem with "staying put" in space

Everything in space is orbiting something, and [orbital speed depends on
distance](/posts/03-orbital-speed-and-escape/): closer means faster, farther
means slower. So a satellite a little closer to the Sun than Earth would orbit
*faster* and pull ahead of us; one a little farther would lag behind. There's
seemingly no way to keep a fixed position relative to the Earth as it goes around
the Sun.

Except there is — at five precise spots. At these points the combined pull of the
**two** big bodies provides exactly the force needed to orbit at *their* shared
rate, so a small third body holds station with the pair, going around the Sun
once a year right alongside us.

## Meet the five points

Joseph-Louis Lagrange found all five in 1772. Three of them lie on the line
running through both bodies; the other two sit off to the sides.

- **L1** — between the two bodies. (For Sun–Earth, this is sunward of us — a
  perfect perch for solar observatories that need an uninterrupted view of the
  Sun.)
- **L2** — just *beyond* the smaller body, on the far side from the larger one.
  Cold, stable-ish, and always pointing away from the Sun: the home of the James
  Webb Space Telescope.
- **L3** — on the *opposite* side of the larger body, forever hidden behind it.
- **L4 and L5** — sixty degrees ahead of and behind the smaller body, each one
  forming a perfect **equilateral triangle** with the two bodies.

## Stable hills and unstable saddles

Not all five points are equally comfortable to sit at.

- **L4 and L5 are stable** (as long as the big body is at least ~25× the smaller
  one). Nudge an object there and it gently loops back. That's why they collect
  debris naturally: Jupiter's **Trojan asteroids** are tens of thousands of rocks
  pooled in the Sun–Jupiter L4 and L5 points, shepherded there over billions of
  years.
- **L1, L2 and L3 are unstable** — like balancing a marble on a saddle. A
  spacecraft parked there will slowly drift off, so it must nudge itself back
  every few weeks with tiny **station-keeping** burns. It's still wildly cheaper
  than fighting gravity from scratch.

## Why we build telescopes out there

Sun–Earth **L2**, about 1.5 million km beyond us, is prime real estate. A
telescope there:

- keeps the Sun, Earth and Moon all bunched together in one patch of sky, so a
  single sunshield can block all three and let the instruments run brutally cold;
- orbits the Sun in lockstep with Earth, so it never falls behind or drifts away;
- enjoys a clear, stable view of deep space with no planet looming nearby.

The James Webb Space Telescope lives there now, as did Gaia and Planck before it.
**L1**, on the sunward side, hosts Sun-watchers like SOHO and DSCOVR.

## The view from here

Starting from a single idea — gravity weakening as $1/r^2$ — we have built our
way through orbits, escape, ellipses, transfers, slingshots, and now to the quiet
equilibrium points where spacecraft can simply *rest* between worlds:

- **Gravity assists** borrow a planet's motion for free speed.
- **Lagrange points** are the natural parking spots of any orbiting pair — five
  places where the whole tug-of-war of gravity finally balances out.

Everything fancier in spaceflight is built from exactly these pieces. Thanks for
falling around the planet — and now, for hovering perfectly still beside it.
