We can now name the [orbital regimes](/posts/08-orbital-regimes/) and explain the
[geostationary belt](/posts/09-geostationary-orbits/). But how does the
[live viewer](/viewer/) actually *know* where ten thousand satellites are? It
downloads a tiny text file for each one. To read it, you need the language of
**orbital elements**.

## Six numbers fix an orbit

An orbit floating in 3D space is fully described by just six quantities — the
classical **Keplerian elements**. Two set the ellipse's size and shape, three
orient that ellipse in space, and one says where the satellite currently sits on
it:

- **Semi-major axis ($a$)** — the orbit's size, which (via
  [Kepler](/posts/04-keplers-laws/)) also fixes its period.
- **Eccentricity ($e$)** — its shape, from a perfect circle ($e = 0$) to an
  ever-more-stretched ellipse.
- **Inclination ($i$)** — how tilted the orbital plane is relative to the
  equator. (The star of the [next post](/posts/12-inclination-and-ground-tracks/).)
- **Right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, $\Omega$)** — the swivel of the
  plane: where the orbit crosses the equator going north.
- **Argument of perigee ($\omega$)** — the rotation of the ellipse *within* its
  plane: which way the low point (perigee) faces.
- **True/mean anomaly** — the satellite's position along the orbit right now.

The first two shape the orbit; the next three aim it; the last is the clock hand.

## The TLE: an orbit as two lines of text

Those elements are shipped around the world in a 1960s-era format called a
**two-line element set**, or **TLE**. It is dense, fixed-width, and at first
glance unreadable — but every field is in there. The block above highlights the
key ones; hover them to see what each cryptic number means.

Line 2 carries the geometry: **inclination**, **RAAN**, **eccentricity**
(written without its leading "0."), **argument of perigee**, **mean anomaly**, and
**mean motion** — the number of orbits per day, which is just the period in
disguise. Line 1 carries bookkeeping: the catalog number and, crucially, the
**epoch** — the exact moment those numbers were measured.

## Where the viewer gets them

The viewer pulls fresh TLEs straight from
[CelesTrak](https://celestrak.org/), which republishes the orbital data the
U.S. Space Force tracks for tens of thousands of objects. Each satellite's
detail panel in the viewer — inclination, period, apogee, perigee, eccentricity —
is simply these same elements, decoded from its TLE and shown in friendlier
units.

But a TLE is only a **snapshot** at its epoch. Satellites drift: the atmosphere
drags on them, Earth's lumpy gravity tugs at them, the Sun and Moon pull. So the
numbers slowly go stale, which is why fresh sets are published constantly.

That raises the real question behind the live globe: given a snapshot from some
moment in the past, how do you compute where the satellite is *right now*? That is
the job of a propagation model — and it is what we turn to next.
