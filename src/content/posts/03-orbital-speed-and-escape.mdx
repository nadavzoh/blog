Last time we derived the speed needed for a circular orbit:

$$
v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}
$$

Now let's make it concrete, and then ask the natural follow-up question: what if
you want to leave the planet *entirely* and never come back?

## From orbiting to escaping

Recall gravity never truly reaches zero — it just fades with distance. To escape
a planet, you don't need to "outrun" gravity all at once; you need enough speed
that gravity can keep slowing you down forever **without ever stopping you.**

Working that out with energy (the kinetic energy you start with must cover all
the gravitational energy you'd ever lose) gives the **escape velocity**:

$$
v_{\text{escape}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\; v_{\text{orbit}}
$$

That factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ is a lovely result: **escape speed is just
about 41% faster than orbital speed** at any given altitude. If you're already
circling a planet, you're already most of the way to leaving it.

A few things worth noticing as you experiment:

- **Earth at ~400 km** (where the ISS lives) needs about $7.7\ \text{km/s}$ to
  orbit — roughly 27,000 km/h. Space is less about going *up* and more about
  going *sideways* outrageously fast.
- **The Moon** is gentle: its low mass means low orbital and escape speeds. This
  is exactly why the Apollo lunar module could take off from the Moon with a
  tiny engine.
- **Jupiter** is brutal: enormous mass means enormous speeds. Escaping Jupiter is
  far harder than escaping Earth.
- For **every** body, the escape number is always $\sqrt{2}$ times the orbit
  number. Switch worlds and the ratio holds.

## Where we're headed

So far we've only talked about perfect circles. But real orbits — comets,
planets, spacecraft — are usually **stretched ovals** called ellipses. To
understand those, we turn to the astronomer who figured out their rules a
*lifetime before* Newton explained why: Johannes Kepler.
