In the [last post](/posts/10-orbital-elements-and-tles/) we saw that a TLE is a
snapshot: a set of [orbital elements](/posts/10-orbital-elements-and-tles/)
measured at one instant, the **epoch**. But the satellite never stops moving, and
the orbit itself slowly changes. To draw a satellite *now*, you need to carry
that snapshot forward in time. That step is called **propagation**, and the
standard model for TLEs is **SGP4**.

## Why you can't just use Kepler

If Earth were a perfect sphere and nothing else pulled on a satellite, a pure
[Keplerian orbit](/posts/04-keplers-laws/) would last forever and simple geometry
would place it for any future time. Reality is messier:

- **Earth is not round.** Its equatorial bulge makes gravity slightly uneven,
  which steadily swivels the orbital plane (it drags the RAAN and argument of
  perigee around over days and weeks).
- **The atmosphere drags.** In LEO, faint wisps of air bleed energy, lowering and
  shrinking the orbit over time.
- **The Sun and Moon tug**, and sunlight itself nudges the satellite.

A TLE quietly bakes these perturbing effects into its averaged elements. You
cannot feed those numbers into a textbook ellipse and get the right answer — they
were *designed* to be read by a matching model.

## What SGP4 does

**SGP4** (Simplified General Perturbations 4) is that matching model. Hand it a
TLE and a target time, and it returns the satellite's position and velocity,
having accounted for the dominant perturbations above. The recipe is fixed and
public, so anyone — mission control or a web page — gets the same answer:

1. Read the elements and epoch from the TLE.
2. Compute how far ahead of the epoch you want a position (could be seconds or
   days).
3. Apply SGP4's perturbation formulas to advance the orbit to that moment.
4. Out comes a 3D position in an Earth-centred inertial frame.
5. Spin that into Earth-fixed coordinates to know which point on the globe it is
   over.

TLEs and SGP4 are a **matched pair**: the format and the model were built
together, which is why "a TLE" almost always implies "propagate it with SGP4."

## The viewer, made literal

This is the entire engine behind the [live viewer](/viewer/). For every satellite
it fetches a TLE from CelesTrak, runs SGP4 **in your browser** (via the
`satellite.js` library) to get a fresh position for the current simulated time,
and draws it on the globe — thousands of times, several times a second. The
time-rate slider simply asks SGP4 for positions further into the future, faster.

## The limits

SGP4 is fast and good, but it is an **approximation** tuned for TLE accuracy —
typically right to about a kilometre near the epoch, drifting as the snapshot
ages. For pin-point work (docking, precise pass timing) operators use heavier
models and fresher data, and add effects the viewer skips: your exact location,
the finite speed of light, and atmospheric refraction bending the line of sight.

For *seeing the shape of near-Earth space*, though, it is perfect. One last piece
remains: why the orbits lean the way they do across the map. That is inclination,
and the trails it leaves — ground tracks.
