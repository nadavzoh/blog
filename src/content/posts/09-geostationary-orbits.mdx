In the [last post](/posts/08-orbital-regimes/) we met the three orbital regimes
and saw that **GEO** is a single thin ring where satellites seem to hang
motionless. That trick — a satellite that never moves across your sky — powers
satellite TV, weather imaging, and a huge slice of global communications. Let's
see exactly why it works, and why it happens at one specific height.

## One altitude, one period

The whole idea rests on [Kepler's third law](/posts/04-keplers-laws/), which ties
an orbit's size to its period. For a circular orbit of radius $r$ around Earth:

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}
$$

where $\mu = GM$ is Earth's gravitational parameter. The period grows with
altitude, smoothly and predictably. So there is **exactly one** altitude where
the period equals the time it takes Earth to spin once. Match those two and the
satellite turns in perfect lockstep with the ground below it.

That crossover lands at about **35,786 km** above the surface (a radius of
~42,164 km from Earth's centre). Below it, the satellite outruns the planet and
drifts **east**; above it, it lags behind and slips **west**. Only at the magic
altitude does it hold station.

## Sidereal vs. solar: the 23h 56m subtlety

Notice the target period is **23 hours 56 minutes**, not a round 24 hours. That
is the *sidereal* day — one full spin of Earth relative to the distant stars. The
familiar 24-hour *solar* day is slightly longer because Earth also moves a little
along its orbit around the Sun each day, so it has to turn an extra ~1° to bring
the Sun back overhead. A satellite keeps step with the **stars-frame** rotation,
so the sidereal day is the number that matters.

## Geostationary vs. geosynchronous

Two words that get muddled:

- **Geosynchronous** — any orbit with a one-day period. It returns to the same
  spot at the same time each day, but if it is **tilted** (inclined) it traces a
  figure-eight in the sky over a day rather than sitting still.
- **Geostationary** — the special case that is *also* directly over the equator
  (zero inclination) and circular. Only this one truly **hovers**.

That equator requirement is why the geostationary belt is a single shared ring of
real estate — and why slots in it are carefully allocated.

## Why we put things there

A geostationary satellite buys you two superpowers:

- **Fixed aim.** A ground dish can point at one patch of sky and never move,
  which is exactly what made home satellite TV practical.
- **A constant hemisphere view.** From 35,786 km up, one satellite sees nearly
  half the planet at once. Three of them, spaced 120° apart, blanket almost the
  entire globe — the backbone of weather monitoring and global relays.

The cost is distance: signals travel ~36,000 km each way, adding a noticeable
delay, and reaching that altitude takes far more energy than LEO. That trade-off
is exactly why the [low shells](/posts/08-orbital-regimes/) stay so crowded while
the GEO ring holds a smaller, premium crowd.

In the viewer, GEO is the faraway ring of objects that barely seem to drift. Now
you know they are not frozen — they are orbiting at over 3 km/s, just perfectly
matched to the planet turning beneath them.
