We've been assuming perfectly circular orbits to keep the math friendly. Time to
come clean: **almost no orbit is a perfect circle.** Most are *ellipses* —
stretched ovals. A century before Newton explained the *why*, Johannes Kepler
worked out the *what*, purely from meticulous observations of the planets.

He distilled it all into three laws.

## First Law: orbits are ellipses

> The orbit of each planet is an ellipse, with the Sun at one **focus**.

An ellipse has two special interior points called foci. The planet does **not**
orbit the *centre* of the ellipse — it orbits one of the foci, where the Sun
sits. This means a planet's distance from the Sun changes over the year:

- **Perihelion** — the closest point to the Sun.
- **Aphelion** — the farthest point.

A circle is just the special case of an ellipse where both foci merge into the
middle.

## Second Law: planets sweep equal areas in equal times

> A line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal **areas** in equal
> **times**.

This is Kepler's clever way of describing speed. Imagine the line connecting the
planet to the Sun as it slides around the orbit. For it to cover the same *area*
each month, the planet must move **fast when it's close** to the Sun and **slow
when it's far.** Comets show this dramatically: they whip around the Sun in days,
then crawl through the outer solar system for decades.

This "equal areas" rule is really **conservation of angular momentum** in
disguise: with gravity pulling straight toward the Sun, there is nothing to
twist the orbit, so the product of distance and sideways speed stays fixed —
closer in forces faster motion, farther out forces slower.

## Third Law: bigger orbits take longer — predictably

> The square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its
> orbital size (semi-major axis).

In symbols, with period $T$ and semi-major axis $a$ (half the long diameter of
the ellipse):

$$
T^2 \propto a^3
$$

If you measure $T$ in Earth-years and $a$ in **astronomical units** (1 AU = the
Earth–Sun distance), the proportionality becomes a clean equality:

$$
T^2 = a^3
$$

## The bridge to Newton

Kepler *described* these patterns but couldn't say *why* they were true. Newton
later showed that all three laws fall straight out of his single inverse-square
law of gravity from [post one](/posts/01-what-is-gravity/). The same $1/r^2$ that
weakened the force on our slider is what bends orbits into ellipses and sets
their rhythm.

Now that we understand the shape and timing of orbits, we can do something
genuinely useful: move a spacecraft from one orbit to another as cheaply as
possible.
