So far this series has been about the *physics* of a single orbit — gravity,
speed, ellipses, and how to move between orbits. Now we turn the telescope
around and look at the **real population** of things circling Earth, the very
objects you can watch in the [live satellite viewer](/viewer/).

Open that viewer and one thing jumps out immediately: satellites are not
scattered evenly. They pile up into a handful of distinct **shells**. Those
shells have names, and knowing them is the key to reading everything else.

## Why altitude decides everything

From [post three](/posts/03-orbital-speed-and-escape/) we know orbital speed
depends only on distance from Earth's centre:

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}
$$

And from [Kepler's third law](/posts/04-keplers-laws/), the higher you orbit, the
**longer** each lap takes. So altitude sets a satellite's speed, its period, how
much of the planet it can see at once, and how hard it was to haul up there. Pick
an altitude and you have essentially chosen the satellite's whole personality.

Engineers carve the result into three broad regimes.

## LEO — Low Earth Orbit

Roughly **160 to 2,000 km** up. This is the basement: cheapest to reach, so it is
by far the most crowded. A LEO satellite laps the planet in about **90 minutes**,
screaming along at nearly 8 km/s, and sees only a small patch of ground at a
time — so global coverage needs *many* of them.

This is where the crewed space stations live, where Hubble looks outward, and
where the giant internet constellations like **Starlink** spread into the dense
bands you can see fanning out in the viewer's *LEO structure* mode.

## MEO — Medium Earth Orbit

The wide middle ground, from **2,000 km up to about 35,786 km**. It is mostly
empty, with one famous set of tenants: the **navigation constellations**. GPS,
Galileo and GLONASS sit here at around 20,000 km, where a single satellite takes
roughly **half a day** to orbit and stays above your horizon long enough for a
receiver to lock several at once and triangulate your position.

## GEO — Geostationary Orbit

A single, razor-thin ring at exactly **35,786 km** directly above the equator. A
satellite here completes one lap in precisely one Earth day, so it appears to
**hang motionless** over a fixed point on the ground. That is gold for weather
imagers and TV/communications relays, which can then point a dish at one spot in
the sky and never move it. We will spend the [next post](/posts/09-geostationary-orbits/)
on exactly why this altitude is so special.

## Reading the shells in the viewer

This is the missing manual for that globe. When you switch between *LEO normal*
(true altitudes) and *LEO structure* (a compressed, log-style scale), you are
looking at exactly these regimes:

- the tight, busy band hugging the surface is **LEO**;
- the lonely middle is **MEO**, with the GPS family looping through it;
- the far-out ring of objects that barely seem to move is **GEO**.

Same data, two mappings — precisely the trade-off the diagram above makes to keep
LEO legible next to a GEO ring almost six Earth-radii out.
