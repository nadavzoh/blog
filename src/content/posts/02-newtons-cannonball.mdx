In [the last post](/posts/01-what-is-gravity/) we learned that gravity is always
pulling you toward the centre of a planet. So a natural question is: why doesn't
the International Space Station just fall down?

The surprising answer: **it is falling.** Constantly. It just keeps missing the
Earth.

## A cannon on an impossibly tall mountain

Newton imagined firing a cannonball horizontally from a mountain so tall it
pokes above the atmosphere (so there's no air to slow things down).

- Fire it **slowly**, and it arcs downward and crashes into the ground — just
  like throwing a ball.
- Fire it **faster**, and it travels farther before landing, because the ground
  curves away beneath it.
- Fire it at *just the right speed*, and the ground curves away **exactly as
  fast as the ball falls.** Now it never lands. It falls around the planet
  forever. **That is an orbit.**
- Fire it **too fast**, and it climbs away and never comes back — it *escapes*.

## Orbiting is continuous free-fall

This is worth pausing on, because it's the single most counter-intuitive idea in
spaceflight. Astronauts float **not** because there's no gravity up there — at
the ISS's altitude gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. They
float because they and their spacecraft are *all falling together* at the same
rate. Free-fall feels like weightlessness.

## The speed that keeps you in orbit

For a perfectly circular orbit, there's exactly one speed that works at a given
distance $r$ from the centre of a planet with mass $M$. We can find it by setting
gravity equal to the "centripetal" pull needed to bend a path into a circle:

$$
\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r}
$$

The satellite's own mass $m$ cancels from both sides — a heavy satellite and a
light one orbit at the *same* speed. Solving for $v$:

$$
v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}
$$

Two things fall out of this neat formula:

1. **Lower orbits are faster.** Smaller $r$ means a bigger $v$. The ISS skims
   close to Earth and races around in about 90 minutes.
2. **It depends on the planet, not on you.** Only the planet's mass $M$ and your
   distance $r$ matter.

In the next post we'll put real numbers into this formula, and discover its
close cousin — the speed you need to break free of a planet entirely.
