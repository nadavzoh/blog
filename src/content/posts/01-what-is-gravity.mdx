Welcome to **Orbital Notes**. Over this short series we'll build up — from
absolute scratch — an understanding of how spacecraft, moons, and planets move
through space. No prior physics needed. We start at the very beginning: the
force behind everything that follows.

## Gravity is just attraction

Every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass. You pull on the
Earth, and the Earth pulls on you. The reason *you* notice the Earth's pull (and
not the other way around) is that the Earth is enormously more massive.

Isaac Newton wrote this down in 1687 as the **law of universal gravitation**:

$$
F = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}
$$

Let's decode every symbol, because this one equation powers the entire series:

- $F$ — the gravitational force between the two objects.
- $m_1$ and $m_2$ — the two masses (say, a planet and a satellite).
- $r$ — the distance **between their centres**.
- $G$ — the *gravitational constant*, a tiny fixed number,
  $G \approx 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$, that sets the overall strength of gravity.

## The most important part: that little $r^2$

Notice the distance $r$ is **squared** and sits on the *bottom* of the fraction.
That single detail shapes everything in space. It means gravity follows an
**inverse-square law**: double your distance and the force doesn't halve — it
drops to a *quarter*. Triple it, and you're down to a *ninth*.

See how steeply the curve falls near the surface and then flattens out? Gravity
never quite reaches zero — it just gets weaker and weaker forever. That "weak but
never gone" tail is exactly what lets distant moons stay bound to their planets.

## Why this matters for orbits

Here's the key idea to carry forward: gravity is **always pulling you toward the
centre** of whatever massive body you're near. It never pushes. So if everything
is being pulled *down*, how does anything stay *up* in space?

That puzzle — how to fall without ever hitting the ground — is the secret of
orbits, and it's exactly where we go next.
