In the [last post](/posts/05-hohmann-transfers/) we spent fuel — two careful
burns — to climb from one orbit to another. But the giants of the outer solar
system are so far away that no chemical rocket carries enough propellant to get
there quickly on burns alone. So how did Voyager reach Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus
*and* Neptune on one tank?

It cheated, beautifully. It used **gravity assists** — also called *slingshots*
— to pick up speed from the planets themselves.

## A free lunch that isn't really free

Here's the puzzle. Gravity is a *conservative* force: as a spacecraft falls
toward a planet it speeds up, and as it climbs back out the planet's gravity
slows it by exactly the same amount. From the **planet's point of view**, the
craft leaves a flyby with the very same speed it came in with — only pointing in
a new direction. Nothing gained.

The magic appears only when you remember one thing: **the planet is moving.** It
is racing around the Sun at tens of kilometres per second. So while the flyby is
"free" in the planet's frame, in the **Sun's frame** that change of direction
can add up to a huge change in speed.

## Bouncing off a moving wall

The cleanest way to feel it: imagine throwing a ball at a wall. It bounces back
with the same speed. Now imagine the wall is a train moving toward you. The ball
comes back *faster* — it stole some of the train's motion. A planet is that
train, and its gravity is the (very gentle, curved) wall.

The amount you gain depends on how sharply the planet bends your path. A distant,
glancing pass barely turns you and barely helps. A close pass that swings you
right around hands you the maximum boost — up to roughly **twice the planet's
orbital speed.**

## Conservation isn't broken

If the spacecraft gains energy, where does it come from? From the planet. The
craft tugs back on the planet just as hard as the planet pulls on it, so the
planet *loses* exactly the orbital energy the spacecraft gains. Jupiter is so
overwhelmingly massive that the cost is unmeasurable — its year lengthens by
less than the width of a proton — but the bookkeeping balances perfectly. No
law is broken; we're just moving energy from a planet to a probe.

## Why mission designers love it

Gravity assists turn the solar system into a network of free on-ramps:

- **Voyager 2** chained Jupiter → Saturn → Uranus → Neptune, each flyby flinging
  it onward to the next world.
- **Cassini** took the scenic route to Saturn — Venus, Venus, Earth, Jupiter —
  trading time for the speed it couldn't carry as fuel.
- Probes can also fly *in front* of a planet to **lose** speed on purpose, which
  is how missions like Parker Solar Probe spiral inward toward the Sun.

The price is patience: lining up the planets and looping the long way around
costs years. But delta-v you don't have to carry is delta-v you don't have to
launch — and that is often the difference between a possible mission and an
impossible one.

## Where to next

We've now bent our path *around* a planet to gain speed. Next we'll find the
opposite kind of place: a handful of points where gravity and orbital motion
balance so perfectly that a spacecraft can simply **park** and stay put.
