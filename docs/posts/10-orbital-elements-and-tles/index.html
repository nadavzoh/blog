Orbital Elements & TLEs: An Orbit in Six Numbers

A whole orbit — its size, shape, tilt and where the satellite is on it — fits in a handful of numbers. Meet the orbital elements and the cryptic two-line format the viewer downloads.

March 10, 2025 #satellites #orbital-elements #tle #data

We can now name the orbital regimes and explain the geostationary belt. But how does the live viewer actually know where ten thousand satellites are? It downloads a tiny text file for each one. To read it, you need the language of orbital elements.

Six numbers fix an orbit

An orbit floating in 3D space is fully described by just six quantities — the classical Keplerian elements. Two set the ellipse’s size and shape, three orient that ellipse in space, and one says where the satellite currently sits on it:

  • Semi-major axis (aaa) — the orbit’s size, which (via Kepler) also fixes its period.
  • Eccentricity (eee) — its shape, from a perfect circle (e=0e = 0e=0) to an ever-more-stretched ellipse.
  • Inclination (iii) — how tilted the orbital plane is relative to the equator. (The star of the next post.)
  • Right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, Ω\OmegaΩ) — the swivel of the plane: where the orbit crosses the equator going north.
  • Argument of perigee (ω\omegaω) — the rotation of the ellipse within its plane: which way the low point (perigee) faces.
  • True/mean anomaly — the satellite’s position along the orbit right now.

The first two shape the orbit; the next three aim it; the last is the clock hand. Drag the sliders below to feel two of them directly — stretching the ellipse with eccentricity and swinging perigee around with the argument of perigee:

The TLE: an orbit as two lines of text

Those elements are shipped around the world in a 1960s-era format called a two-line element set, or TLE. It is dense, fixed-width, and at first glance unreadable — but every field is in there. The block above highlights the key ones; hover them to see what each cryptic number means.

Line 2 carries the geometry: inclination, RAAN, eccentricity (written without its leading “0.”), argument of perigee, mean anomaly, and mean motion — the number of orbits per day, which is just the period in disguise. Line 1 carries bookkeeping: the catalog number and, crucially, the epoch — the exact moment those numbers were measured.

Where the viewer gets them

The viewer pulls fresh TLEs straight from CelesTrak, which republishes the orbital data the U.S. Space Force tracks for tens of thousands of objects. Each satellite’s detail panel in the viewer — inclination, period, apogee, perigee, eccentricity — is simply these same elements, decoded from its TLE and shown in friendlier units.

But a TLE is only a snapshot at its epoch. Satellites drift: the atmosphere drags on them, Earth’s lumpy gravity tugs at them, the Sun and Moon pull. So the numbers slowly go stale, which is why fresh sets are published constantly.

That raises the real question behind the live globe: given a snapshot from some moment in the past, how do you compute where the satellite is right now? That is the job of a propagation model — and it is what we turn to next.
