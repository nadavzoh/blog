Hohmann Transfers: Moving Between Orbits

You are in a low orbit and want a higher one. The cheapest path is half of an ellipse and exactly two engine burns. This is how we reach other planets.

By Orbital Notes #maneuvers #delta-v #mission-design

We’ve reached the payoff. You understand gravity, what an orbit is, how fast you must go, and the elliptical shapes orbits take. Now let’s navigate: how do you move a spacecraft from one circular orbit up to a higher one — using as little fuel as possible?

The classic answer is the Hohmann transfer orbit, and its beauty is that it needs just two engine burns.

Fuel is everything: meet delta-v

In space there’s no air and no friction, so you don’t burn fuel to keep moving — you only burn fuel to change your motion. The total amount of velocity change a mission requires is called its delta-v (written Δv\Delta vΔv). Think of delta-v as the true “distance” between orbits. Every drop of propellant a rocket carries buys a fixed budget of delta-v, so smart mission design means spending it wisely.

How much delta-v a given tank of fuel actually buys is set by Tsiolkovsky’s rocket equation:

Δv=veln⁡ ⁣(m0mf)\Delta v = v_{\text{e}} \ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)Δv=ve​ln(mf​m0​​)

Here vev_{\text{e}}ve​ is the exhaust speed of the engine, m0m_0m0​ the fully fuelled mass, and mfm_fmf​ the mass once the tanks are dry. The logarithm is the cruel part: because delta-v grows only with the log of the mass ratio, each extra bit of delta-v costs exponentially more propellant. That is exactly why mission designers fight so hard to shave delta-v — and why the fuel-sipping maneuvers below matter so much.

The two-burn dance

A Hohmann transfer connects a small circular orbit to a larger one using half of an ellipse that just touches both:

  1. Burn 1 — speed up. A short prograde (forward) burn raises the far side of your orbit until it reaches the target altitude. You’re now coasting along a transfer ellipse, climbing outward and naturally slowing down (remember Kepler’s second law — far means slow).
  2. Coast. Engines off. Gravity does all the work for half an orbit.
  3. Burn 2 — speed up again. Arriving at the high point, you’re moving too slowly for a circular orbit way out here, so a second prograde burn circularises you into the new orbit.

Why “half an ellipse” is the cheap option

It feels backwards that you speed up to climb to a higher orbit, even though higher orbits move slower overall (from post two, v=GM/rv = \sqrt{GM/r}v=GM/r​). The resolution: your first burn trades speed for altitude. As you coast outward, gravity bleeds away that extra speed, so by the time you arrive you’re actually moving slower than before — which is why you need that second burn to top back up.

To put real numbers on those burns we need the speed at any point of an orbit, not just a circular one. That comes from the vis-viva equation, which follows straight from conservation of energy:

v2=GM(2r−1a)v^2 = G M\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)v2=GM(r2​−a1​)

where rrr is the current distance from the planet’s centre and aaa is the semi-major axis of the orbit you’re on. For a circle r=ar = ar=a and it collapses back to v=GM/rv = \sqrt{GM/r}v=GM/r​; for the transfer ellipse, aaa is the average of the inner and outer radii. Each Hohmann burn is just the difference between the circular speed and the transfer-ellipse speed at that radius.

The total cost is the sum of the two burns:

Δvtotal=Δv1+Δv2\Delta v_{\text{total}} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2Δvtotal​=Δv1​+Δv2​

For most journeys between nearby circular orbits, no two-burn maneuver beats the Hohmann transfer on fuel. It’s literally how we send probes to Mars: wait for the planets to line up, burn once to leave Earth’s orbit around the Sun, coast for months along a transfer ellipse, then burn again to arrive.

Where to from here

From a single idea — gravity falling off as 1/r21/r^21/r2 — we’ve built our way to interplanetary travel:

  • Gravity pulls everything toward a centre, weakening with the square of distance.
  • Orbits are continuous free-fall: falling sideways fast enough to miss.
  • Orbital and escape speeds tell us how fast “fast enough” is.
  • Kepler’s laws give orbits their elliptical shape and rhythm.
  • Hohmann transfers let us hop between orbits — and between worlds.

That’s the core of orbital mechanics. But two beautiful tricks remain. The Hohmann transfer spends fuel to climb; next we’ll see how to gain speed for free by swinging past a moving planet.
