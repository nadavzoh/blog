Inclination & Ground Tracks: Why Orbits Lean

Why are some orbits equatorial, some polar, and some tilted just so? Inclination decides — and it draws a signature sine wave on the map called a ground track.

March 24, 2025 #satellites #inclination #ground-track #orbits

We’ve reached the last piece of the puzzle. We can name the regimes, read an orbit’s elements, and propagate a satellite to right now. But why does the viewer show some orbits hugging the equator and others sweeping clear over the poles? The answer is a single element: inclination.

What inclination means

Inclination is the tilt of the orbital plane relative to Earth’s equator.

  • 0° — equatorial: the satellite stays over the equator the whole time.
  • 90° — polar: the orbit passes over both poles, eventually overflying every point on Earth as the planet rotates beneath it.
  • Between — the orbit reaches as far north and south as its inclination, but never higher. A 51.6° orbit (the ISS) never passes over anywhere above 51.6° latitude.

This is a hard geometric ceiling, and it explains a launch fact: you can’t reach an inclination lower than your launch site’s latitude without an expensive plane change. It also shapes constellations — a whole family of orbits at the same inclination weaves the repeating patterns you see draped over the globe.

The ground track

Trace the point on Earth directly beneath a satellite as it orbits and you get a ground track. Because the satellite swings north and south while Earth spins west-to-east underneath, the track draws a leaning sine wave across the map. Its height is set by the inclination; each successive lap is shifted westward because the planet has rotated during one orbit.

A few inclinations earn special names:

  • Equatorial (0°) — a flat line along the equator; where the geostationary belt lives.
  • ISS (51.6°) — high enough to overfly most of the populated world, low enough to launch to easily from several spaceports.
  • Polar (90°) — sweeps pole to pole; perfect for imaging every spot on Earth.
  • Sun-synchronous (~98°) — a slightly retrograde tilt chosen so that Earth’s equatorial bulge slowly rotates the orbit plane by exactly 360° per year. The orbit then keeps a fixed angle to the Sun, so the satellite crosses the equator at the same local solar time on every pass — ideal for imagery with consistent lighting.

Putting it all together

That sun-synchronous trick is the perfect closing note, because it uses the very “imperfection” — Earth’s bulge — that forced us to use SGP4 instead of a textbook ellipse, and turns it into a feature.

Across these five posts we have gone from the satellite viewer feeling like magic to understanding every part of it:

  • satellites live in distinct regimes — LEO, MEO, and the geostationary ring;
  • each orbit is captured by six orbital elements, shipped as a TLE;
  • SGP4 turns that snapshot into a live position, the engine inside the viewer;
  • and inclination tilts the plane, drawing the ground tracks that pattern the whole globe.

Open the viewer once more. None of it is magic now — it’s just gravity, geometry, and a handful of honest numbers, falling around the planet exactly as we’ve described since post one.
