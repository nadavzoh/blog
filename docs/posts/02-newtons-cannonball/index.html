Newton's Cannonball: What an Orbit Actually Is

An orbit is not "zero gravity." It is falling so fast sideways that you keep missing the planet. Fire the cannon and see for yourself.

By Orbital Notes #orbits #newton #free-fall

In the last post we learned that gravity is always pulling you toward the centre of a planet. So a natural question is: why doesn’t the International Space Station just fall down?

The surprising answer: it is falling. Constantly. It just keeps missing the Earth.

A cannon on an impossibly tall mountain

Newton imagined firing a cannonball horizontally from a mountain so tall it pokes above the atmosphere (so there’s no air to slow things down).

  • Fire it slowly, and it arcs downward and crashes into the ground — just like throwing a ball.
  • Fire it faster, and it travels farther before landing, because the ground curves away beneath it.
  • Fire it at just the right speed, and the ground curves away exactly as fast as the ball falls. Now it never lands. It falls around the planet forever. That is an orbit.
  • Fire it too fast, and it climbs away and never comes back — it escapes.

Orbiting is continuous free-fall

This is worth pausing on, because it’s the single most counter-intuitive idea in spaceflight. Astronauts float not because there’s no gravity up there — at the ISS’s altitude gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. They float because they and their spacecraft are all falling together at the same rate. Free-fall feels like weightlessness.

The speed that keeps you in orbit

For a perfectly circular orbit, there’s exactly one speed that works at a given distance rrr from the centre of a planet with mass MMM. We can find it by setting gravity equal to the “centripetal” pull needed to bend a path into a circle:

GMmr2=mv2r\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r}r2GMm​=rmv2​

The satellite’s own mass mmm cancels from both sides — a heavy satellite and a light one orbit at the same speed. Solving for vvv:

vorbit=GMrv_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}vorbit​=rGM​​

Two things fall out of this neat formula:

  1. Lower orbits are faster. Smaller rrr means a bigger vvv. The ISS skims close to Earth and races around in about 90 minutes.
  2. It depends on the planet, not on you. Only the planet’s mass MMM and your distance rrr matter.

In the next post we’ll put real numbers into this formula, and discover its close cousin — the speed you need to break free of a planet entirely.
