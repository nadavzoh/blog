Kepler's Laws: The Shape and Rhythm of Orbits

Real orbits are ellipses, not circles. Three elegant rules, found by staring at planetary data for decades, describe exactly how they move.

January 27, 2025 #kepler #ellipses #planets

We’ve been assuming perfectly circular orbits to keep the math friendly. Time to come clean: almost no orbit is a perfect circle. Most are ellipses — stretched ovals. A century before Newton explained the why, Johannes Kepler worked out the what, purely from meticulous observations of the planets.

He distilled it all into three laws.

First Law: orbits are ellipses

The orbit of each planet is an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.

An ellipse has two special interior points called foci. The planet does not orbit the centre of the ellipse — it orbits one of the foci, where the Sun sits. This means a planet’s distance from the Sun changes over the year:

  • Perihelion — the closest point to the Sun.
  • Aphelion — the farthest point.

A circle is just the special case of an ellipse where both foci merge into the middle.

Second Law: planets sweep equal areas in equal times

A line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

This is Kepler’s clever way of describing speed. Imagine the line connecting the planet to the Sun as it slides around the orbit. For it to cover the same area each month, the planet must move fast when it’s close to the Sun and slow when it’s far. Comets show this dramatically: they whip around the Sun in days, then crawl through the outer solar system for decades.

Third Law: bigger orbits take longer — predictably

The square of a planet’s orbital period is proportional to the cube of its orbital size (semi-major axis).

In symbols, with period TTT and semi-major axis aaa (half the long diameter of the ellipse):

T2∝a3T^2 \propto a^3T2∝a3

If you measure TTT in Earth-years and aaa in astronomical units (1 AU = the Earth–Sun distance), the proportionality becomes a clean equality:

T2=a3T^2 = a^3T2=a3

The bridge to Newton

Kepler described these patterns but couldn’t say why they were true. Newton later showed that all three laws fall straight out of his single inverse-square law of gravity from post one. The same 1/r21/r^21/r2 that weakened the force on our slider is what bends orbits into ellipses and sets their rhythm.

Now that we understand the shape and timing of orbits, we can do something genuinely useful: move a spacecraft from one orbit to another as cheaply as possible.
