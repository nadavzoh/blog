Orbital Regimes: LEO, MEO and GEO

Satellites do not sit just anywhere. They cluster into a few altitude shells, each with its own speed, purpose, and crowd. Meet the neighbourhoods of near-Earth space.

By Orbital Notes #satellites #leo #geo #orbits

So far this series has been about the physics of a single orbit — gravity, speed, ellipses, and how to move between orbits. Now we turn the telescope around and look at the real population of things circling Earth, the very objects you can watch in the live satellite viewer.

Open that viewer and one thing jumps out immediately: satellites are not scattered evenly. They pile up into a handful of distinct shells. Those shells have names, and knowing them is the key to reading everything else.

Why altitude decides everything

From post three we know orbital speed depends only on distance from Earth’s centre:

v=GMrv = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}v=rGM​​

And from Kepler’s third law, the higher you orbit, the longer each lap takes. So altitude sets a satellite’s speed, its period, how much of the planet it can see at once, and how hard it was to haul up there. Pick an altitude and you have essentially chosen the satellite’s whole personality.

Engineers carve the result into three broad regimes.

LEO — Low Earth Orbit

Roughly 160 to 2,000 km up. This is the basement: cheapest to reach, so it is by far the most crowded. A LEO satellite laps the planet in about 90 minutes, screaming along at nearly 8 km/s, and sees only a small patch of ground at a time — so global coverage needs many of them.

This is where the crewed space stations live, where Hubble looks outward, and where the giant internet constellations like Starlink spread into the dense bands you can see fanning out in the viewer’s Shell view mode.

MEO — Medium Earth Orbit

The wide middle ground, from 2,000 km up to about 35,786 km. It is mostly empty, with one famous set of tenants: the navigation constellations. GPS, Galileo and GLONASS sit here at around 20,000 km, where a single satellite takes roughly half a day to orbit and stays above your horizon long enough for a receiver to lock several at once and triangulate your position.

GEO — Geostationary Orbit

A single, razor-thin ring at exactly 35,786 km directly above the equator. A satellite here completes one lap in precisely one Earth day, so it appears to hang motionless over a fixed point on the ground. That is gold for weather imagers and TV/communications relays, which can then point a dish at one spot in the sky and never move it. We will spend the next post on exactly why this altitude is so special.

Reading the shells in the viewer

This is the missing manual for that globe. The viewer offers two view modes, and the names describe exactly what each one does to altitude:

  • True scale draws every satellite at its real altitude. It is honest, but because LEO is only a few hundred kilometres up while GEO sits almost six Earth radii out, the low-orbit shells get squashed into a thin, crowded crust against the globe.
  • Shell view keeps the same data but remaps altitude onto a logarithmic scale, stretching the first few thousand kilometres so those packed low-orbit shells fan out into separate, readable bands. This spacing is a visual aid, not a real distance — nothing is actually that far apart. It exists purely to make the shell structure legible.

The shells themselves are real: satellites genuinely cluster into discrete altitude bands (the Starlink shells, the Sun-synchronous imaging band, the GPS ring, the GEO belt). Shell view just exaggerates the gaps between them so you can tell them apart. In either mode you are looking at exactly these regimes:

  • the tight, busy band hugging the surface is LEO;
  • the lonely middle is MEO, with the GPS family looping through it;
  • the far-out ring of objects that barely seem to move is GEO.

To make the individual shells even easier to read, the viewer colour-codes every dot by category — Starlink, OneWeb, space stations, navigation (GPS/GLONASS/Galileo/BeiDou), Iridium, and a catch-all “other”. The colour key beneath the controls lists whatever categories are in the group you loaded, so a single Starlink shell or the GPS ring jumps out as one solid colour instead of a sea of identical dots.

Same data, two mappings — precisely the trade-off the diagram above makes to keep LEO legible next to a GEO ring almost six Earth-radii out.
