Orbital Speed & Escape Velocity

How fast is "fast enough" to orbit — and how much faster must you go to leave a world behind forever? Two formulas, one slider.

January 20, 2025 #velocity #escape #energy

Last time we derived the speed needed for a circular orbit:

vorbit=GMrv_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}vorbit​=rGM​​

Now let’s make it concrete, and then ask the natural follow-up question: what if you want to leave the planet entirely and never come back?

From orbiting to escaping

Recall gravity never truly reaches zero — it just fades with distance. To escape a planet, you don’t need to “outrun” gravity all at once; you need enough speed that gravity can keep slowing you down forever without ever stopping you.

Working that out with energy (the kinetic energy you start with must cover all the gravitational energy you’d ever lose) gives the escape velocity:

vescape=2GMr=2  vorbitv_{\text{escape}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\; v_{\text{orbit}}vescape​=r2GM​​=2​vorbit​

That factor of 2≈1.41\sqrt{2} \approx 1.412​≈1.41 is a lovely result: escape speed is just about 41% faster than orbital speed at any given altitude. If you’re already circling a planet, you’re already most of the way to leaving it.

Play with real worlds

A few things worth noticing as you experiment:

  • Earth at ~400 km (where the ISS lives) needs about 7.7 km/s7.7\ \text{km/s}7.7 km/s to orbit — roughly 27,000 km/h. Space is less about going up and more about going sideways outrageously fast.
  • The Moon is gentle: its low mass means low orbital and escape speeds. This is exactly why the Apollo lunar module could take off from the Moon with a tiny engine.
  • Jupiter is brutal: enormous mass means enormous speeds. Escaping Jupiter is far harder than escaping Earth.
  • For every body, the escape number is always 2\sqrt{2}2​ times the orbit number. Switch worlds and the ratio holds.

Where we’re headed

So far we’ve only talked about perfect circles. But real orbits — comets, planets, spacecraft — are usually stretched ovals called ellipses. To understand those, we turn to the astronomer who figured out their rules a lifetime before Newton explained why: Johannes Kepler.
